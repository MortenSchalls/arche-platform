Cabotage, explained

A trip such as London to Nice, on to Paris and back to London looks simple. Whether one aircraft may fly the Nice to Paris leg depends on where the aircraft and its operator come from. This is cabotage, and getting it wrong can ground a flight.

What cabotage means

Cabotage is carrying paying passengers between two points inside one country. Under Article 7 of the Chicago Convention of 19441, each contracting State may refuse aircraft of other States permission to take on passengers, mail or cargo for payment in its territory and carry them to another point within its territory1. Countries use this right to keep domestic flying for their own airlines and operators.

Article 51 of the same convention lets non-scheduled aircraft fly into a country and stop for non-traffic purposes: aircraft not on scheduled international services may fly into or non-stop across another contracting State and make stops for non-traffic purposes, subject to the Convention and to the right of the State flown over to require a landing1. Flying into a country is therefore a different question from flying between two of its airports.

The United States

US law is clear. Under 49 U.S.C. § 41703(c)2, a foreign civil aircraft may take on passengers or cargo for compensation at one place in the United States for another place in the United States only if specifically authorised under 49 U.S.C. § 40109(g), or under regulations for foreign aircraft leased or chartered without crew to US air carriers2. So we put a leg between two US airports on a US operator, even when the rest of the trip is flown by a foreign aircraft.

The European Union

In Europe the rule works through licensing. Article 15(1) of Regulation (EC) No 1008/20083 says: “Community air carriers shall be entitled to operate intra-Community air services3.” The right to fly between EU airports belongs to carriers licensed in the EU. For a leg between two EU airports we therefore book an operator licensed in the EU, unless the authority concerned has given a specific permission.

Everywhere else

Rules differ from country to country, and the consequences are serious: most countries prohibit cabotage for hire, and some also bar carrying non-paying passengers between two of their own airports4, and in Europe and Canada, aircraft can be seized when a cabotage violation is found4. We check every leg of every trip against the rules of the country it is flown in before we send you a quote.

How we plan around it

When a trip has a domestic leg abroad, we either book an operator from that country for the whole trip, split the trip between two operators with a smooth handover, or change the order of the legs so that no leg breaks the rules. You see the plan, and the reason for it, in your quote.

Questions we are asked

Can a foreign private jet fly me between two cities in the United States?

Generally not for payment. Under 49 U.S.C. § 41703(c)2, a foreign civil aircraft may take on passengers or cargo for compensation at one place in the United States for another place in the United States only if specifically authorised under 49 U.S.C. § 40109(g), or under regulations for foreign aircraft leased or chartered without crew to US air carriers2.

Can a non-EU operator fly a leg between two EU airports?

The right to operate intra-EU air services belongs to EU-licensed carriers (Article 15(1) of Regulation (EC) No 1008/20083). We book an EU-licensed operator for such legs unless a specific permission has been given.

Why does cabotage matter for a multi-city trip?

Because one aircraft may not be allowed to fly every leg. We check each leg before quoting, so the plan you approve is one that can actually fly.
